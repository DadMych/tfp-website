# Blog: retainer or project?

I'm Oleksij van Koeropatkin. I started Northwind in 2017, and the question I still get on a first call is which shape to buy. A retainer, or a project with an end date. People want a rule. There isn't one that ignores the work.

## A project has an ending

A project is for a problem you can point at. A Brand Sprint is that kind of problem: an identity, done, and then we stop. A Website Sprint is that kind of problem: a site, designed, and then we stop. You can say what "finished" means before we begin. The people who do well with a project are the ones who can live with that boundary. When the files are handed over, the engagement is over, and the next thing is a new conversation, not a leftover Tuesday.

That sounds obvious. It isn't how most of the requests arrive. They arrive as "we might also need the sales pages" or "can the logo stretch to the product". Sometimes the honest answer is yes, put it in the project. Sometimes the honest answer is that you have stopped describing a project.

## A retainer is a month, repeated

A retainer is for the work that doesn't have an ending yet. Product design is usually this. The screen you need in November is not the screen you can name in October. A launch slips. A customer conversation changes a flow. You want the same people still in the file, not a new team learning your product from a folder.

You are not buying a pile of named deliverables. You are buying a stretch of attention, every month. The hours on that month are a budget, not a promise of output. A quiet month does not mean we owe you an extra feature. A hard screen does not become smaller because the calendar looked empty. The hours are how we plan. They are not a guarantee that a particular thing will ship, and they are not a stockpile.

Unused hours roll over one month, and then they expire. They are never refunded. I would rather say that on a first call than have someone discover it in month four, with a balance they thought they were saving for a redesign. Use the month. If you already know you will have nothing to give us, say so before we both pretend.

## What goes wrong

The bad project is a retainer in disguise. Scope arrives every week, the end date stays, and everyone acts surprised. The bad retainer is a project in disguise: a fixed list, a fixed date, and irritation when the month doesn't behave like a quote. Both feel tidy in a proposal. Both go badly in the third week.

Two rounds of revision sit on a deliverable either way. They are enough when feedback arrives as one set of decisions. They are not enough when it arrives as a drip. A retainer does not suspend that. The clients who get value from a monthly engagement are the ones who can close a round.

## How to choose

If you can list the work, you probably want a project. If the list is a guess, you probably want a retainer. Software companies often know this about themselves and still ask for the project, because a project looks easier to approve. It is easier to approve. It is worse when the product keeps moving.

If you are unsure, tell us what the coming months actually contain. A chapter wants a project. The rest of the year wants a month, and the hours on that month are a budget, not a promise.
