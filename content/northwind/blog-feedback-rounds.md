# Blog: why we cut revisions from three rounds to two

I'm Varvara Handoga. I design brand work at Northwind, and I was in the files when we still included three rounds. On 1 January 2025 we stopped. Included revisions went from three rounds to two. A year on, I don't want them back.

## What the third round was actually for

In theory the third round was polish. In practice it was where the real feedback finally arrived, because the first two had been used for collecting. Someone would comment on a logo on Monday, someone else would send a different note on Thursday, and the third round would open with a new idea neither of those notes had contained. We would then design the new idea inside a round that was supposed to be the last one. The work got broader, not better.

Three rounds taught people they could wait. If a decision is uncomfortable, there is always another pass. The file stays open, the approver stays undecided, and the thing we were hired to resolve turns into a discussion with no edge.

## What two rounds asks of you

Two rounds is enough when the feedback is one list. Not five threads, not a comment in the file and a different comment in an email and a third one said on a call that nobody wrote down. One list, from the people who are actually allowed to decide, inside the round.

We ask for that feedback within 5 working days. If a round sits in silence for 10 working days, we treat it as accepted. That sounds strict when you read it cold. It is the thing that stops a project spending its last weeks waiting for a reply everyone assumed someone else would send.

Consolidated feedback is not a style preference. It is the reason the second round can be good. If I can see the contradictions in one place, I can tell you about them while there is still a round left to resolve them. If they arrive one by one, each one spends the round, and the contradiction shows up when we are out of rounds.

## Extra rounds still exist

Cutting the included number was not a ban on going further. An extra round is still possible when the work actually needs one. What changed is that it is a decision, not a habit. You notice you're spending it. On the old policy, the third round was spent before anyone noticed, usually on something that should have been in the first list.

I would rather do two sharp rounds than three foggy ones. The foggy ones are how a brand book fills up with decisions nobody quite made. If you want the work to be specific, send one set of notes, on time, and mean them.

People still ask whether the third round can be slipped in quietly. It can't. If the work needs another pass, that pass is an extra round, and we agree it before anyone spends it. Moving from three rounds to two was meant to make that moment visible, not to pretend the work is always finished on the second try. A decision you can see is better than a round you used without noticing.
